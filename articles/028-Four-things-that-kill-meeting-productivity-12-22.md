# Four things that kill meeting productivity

![Four things that kill meeting productivity](../images/028-Four-things-that-kill-meeting-productivity-12-22.jpg)

Business meetings have acquired a reputation as a useless activity that wastes precious time. According to a Harvard Business Review study, 65% of senior managers believe that meetings hinder their work, and 71% consider meetings unproductive and ineffective. The author of the book "How to Conduct Effective, Interesting, and Enjoyable Meetings" Maimi Kanfer Stewart believes that meeting productivity suffers from the following factors.

**You do not verbalize decisions made.**

Many meetings end with the words "Perhaps we have found a solution to the problem," but does everyone understand what decision has been reached? "There is no unified 'we'; not everyone is willing to take responsibility and take the next necessary steps," Stewart believes. "Most likely, everyone at the meeting has their own idea of what needs to be done next, and after the meeting, everyone will go their separate ways with different decisions." The meeting leader should say the following: "Perhaps we need to solve the problem in the following way. Is everyone clear? Can someone repeat and write down what we ultimately decided on?" Employees can suggest different ideas, but someone needs to verbalize and record specific decisions made.

**You invite too many people.**

When you invite employees to a meeting, sometimes the "fear of missing out" syndrome arises, and you invite absolutely everyone. But because of the large number of employees, the meeting can become unproductive. It is better to discuss some details with a few people before the meeting, and then inform those gathered about them. Or have someone comment on the decisions made by email. If you have been invited to a meeting where you feel you are not needed, ask the person who invited you what they expect from you. Explain that you are not trying to avoid attending meetings, but rather want to optimize your time.

**You do not write down important points.**

Some organizations appoint a specific person to record the key points of the meeting. But more often than not, this important task is forgotten. Meeting notes should not look like minutes of a court session, but they should also not contain anything unnecessary. "People will read your notes only if they are concise and relevant," Stewart believes. "If you do not record the conclusions, you force people to attend the meeting simply because otherwise they will not know what important things happened there."

**You do not adhere to the corporate culture.**

A meeting is a good opportunity to let employees feel the corporate culture, but this rarely happens. For example, if you discuss everything openly but do not send anyone the meeting outcomes. Generally, such meetings go poorly because they are organized incorrectly. "When you give people freedom in meetings, you get rid of bureaucracy," Stewart believes. "Some leaders allow their employees to argue, while others think it is inappropriate. But if each meeting follows different rules, people start to get tired." Pay special attention to the people who conduct meetings and the methods they use. But this does not mean that all meetings with employees should always follow the same pattern - for example, a weekly meeting will differ from a company-wide gathering. Think about how to develop a course of action for the project and how to outline the subsequent steps. Use appropriate tools for this, such as Word, Google Docs, or specialized planners.
